# [Psychology] Why You Repeat Habits You Want to Break

Ash: You sit down to write one paragraph. Before the first sentence, your hand reaches for your phone. You check a message, open another app, and suddenly the paragraph is still empty. Why does a behaviour you want to stop keep getting the first move?

Sage: One reason is that the situation has become linked to a familiar response. Repeating an action in a similar setting can make that action easier to trigger without deciding on it from scratch. Today we're going to pull that sequence apart and redesign it. Our example is phone checking during work, but the same questions can help you understand other everyday routines.

Ash: Let's distinguish two things straight away. I might check my phone because I'm waiting for a delivery, which is a deliberate decision. Or I might unlock it, realise I have nothing to check, and put it down. The action looks similar. The second example is closer to what we mean by automatic behaviour.

Sage: Exactly. In their research account, Wendy Wood and David Neal describe habits through learned associations between a context and a response. Goals can get the repetition started. Later, a familiar setting can prompt the response even when your current goal has changed. That helps explain the awkward experience of wanting to work while reaching for a distraction.

Ash: So deciding that the distraction is bad doesn't necessarily remove what triggers it. You can change your opinion about the action faster than you change the routine that keeps producing it.

Sage: Which gives us a useful place to intervene. Let's invent a person called Maya. She edits photographs at her desk. When an image takes a moment to load, she picks up the phone beside her keyboard. Sometimes she finds an interesting message. Sometimes nothing. Either way, waiting has become a moment for checking.

Ash: I could tell Maya to focus harder, but that leaves the phone in the same position and the loading pause in the same place. What would you actually change?

Sage: First I'd make the target precise. For the next editing session, she wants to complete the first image before opening social apps. That's more useful than a promise to become a disciplined person. It identifies the behaviour and the point at which it matters.

Ash: Then I'd look at the physical setup. If she needs the phone for client calls, hiding it in another building isn't a sensible solution. She could keep calls available while moving the phone off the desk, beyond an automatic reach. The change has to fit the job.

Sage: And she needs something to do during that loading pause. She might read the next item in her editing checklist. The replacement is small enough to fit the actual gap. A plan to read a whole chapter whenever an image loads would collapse immediately. A replacement behaviour has to be available when the cue arrives.

Ash: This is where advice about good habits often gets vague. It tells you what an ideal person does, without showing where that action fits into an ordinary afternoon.

Sage: James Clear offers a practical framework in his public explanation of Atomic Habits: cue, craving, response and reward. His four design suggestions are to make a wanted action obvious, attractive, easy and satisfying. Treat that as a way to ask useful design questions. It isn't a requirement to discover four separate conscious thoughts before every action.

Ash: Apply those questions to Maya. The editing checklist is visible beside the screen. Its next step is specific enough to begin. Her phone is less convenient to reach. Finishing the first image gives her a visible unit of progress. Those are changes to the session, not just another promise made before it.

Sage: Notice that the reward question is personal. We shouldn't announce that Maya secretly craves approval or is afraid of success. We invented her example, and real people need to notice what the behaviour does for them. Checking might fill a pause, provide contact, or be something they do without much satisfaction at all.

Ash: And a habit can persist after it stops feeling particularly rewarding. That's why the question isn't always, what wonderful thing am I getting out of this? Sometimes it's simply, in what situation does this familiar action happen?

Sage: Now compare a different person, Leon. He also reaches for his phone while working, but his task is to write a sales email and he doesn't know what to say. Moving the phone may remove a distraction. It doesn't supply the missing knowledge about the customer or the offer.

Ash: Leon needs to make the work itself more executable. Instead of write a brilliant sales email, his first step could be write one sentence describing the customer's problem. If he cannot do that, his next task may be research, not another attempt at forcing concentration.

Sage: That's a distinction worth keeping. An easy distraction and an unclear task can exist together. Change the environment, but also ask whether the intended action is defined and whether you have what you need to perform it. We don't have to turn every difficulty into a personality diagnosis.

Ash: Here's the first recall question. Maya's hand keeps reaching towards the same place during a loading pause. What two parts of that sequence could you redesign?

Sage: The cue arrangement and the response available at that moment. Move the phone out of immediate reach, and put a small relevant next action in view. Those are proposed changes to try, not a guarantee about every person's focus.

Ash: Let's turn to building something wanted. Say I want to read more, but I keep buying books and leaving them closed. How do I move from owning the material to actually beginning?

Sage: Pick one repeatable starting situation. For this example, after putting your breakfast plate away, sit in a particular chair and read the next page of the book already placed there. You have identified an existing event, a location and a small action. You aren't asking yourself to choose a book, find it, choose a time and negotiate a reading target every morning.

Ash: There's a practical detail in that example: the book must already be there. If it is upstairs under another pile, the instruction is technically clear but the setup still creates several opportunities to drift away.

Sage: Right. You can prepare the setting beforehand. But don't confuse making the beginning easy with making your entire ambition tiny. A page is an entry point in this example. Once you're reading, you can continue. If your eventual aim is a demanding book, you will still need longer sessions and sustained thought.

Ash: So a starting habit and the skill you develop during the session are different. Getting to the keyboard doesn't automatically make you a good writer. Opening an accounting textbook doesn't automatically teach accounting. The routine gets you into contact with the work.

Sage: Then the work needs its own structure. For reading, that could mean following an argument carefully and looking up a term you don't understand. For writing, it could mean revising a paragraph against a specific standard. We shouldn't sell a starting routine as the whole achievement.

Ash: What about people whose mornings change? If breakfast is unpredictable, tying everything to one exact morning schedule may be a poor fit.

Sage: Choose a cue that actually exists in your life. Someone might use the moment after returning from work or after settling into a particular train journey. This is a design choice, not a universal prescription. If the anchor rarely happens, choose another. If the action requires quiet and the setting never offers it, choose another setting.

Ash: Second recall question. Why can a goal such as read more leave you stuck, even when you care about it?

Sage: Because it leaves the starting situation and next action unspecified. A concrete cue and a prepared action reduce those decisions. The goal supplies direction; the setup gives you a place to begin.

Ash: How long should I expect this to take before it feels natural? People keep giving a fixed number of days.

Sage: Phillippa Lally and colleagues studied people repeating chosen everyday behaviours and measured how automatic those behaviours felt. The often repeated figure of sixty-six days came from that work, but there was substantial variation. Some estimates extended beyond the observation period. It isn't a deadline after which your routine must switch on. The behaviour and the person matter.

Ash: So day twenty-two is not evidence that I'm broken if I still have to remind myself. And reaching day sixty-six doesn't certify that a difficult activity will now take care of itself.

Sage: Exactly. Pay attention to the action you can repeat, and whether the arrangement is becoming easier to use. You don't need a magical graduation date. If the routine constantly fails at the same point, examine that point. A book you don't understand may need an easier introduction. A work session with no available files needs preparation. More repetition of an impossible setup won't solve those problems.

Ash: Let's make failure concrete. Maya has a good Monday, then on Tuesday takes a client call, keeps the phone beside her and slides back into checking. How does she recover without writing off the whole week?

Sage: She restores the arrangement at the next session: phone in its intended place, editing checklist ready, first image selected. She can also decide where the phone goes after a call. The return from the call was a missing step in her plan. That is a useful repair because it deals with the situation that disrupted the routine.

Ash: That is different from increasing the punishment. She doesn't need to promise three hours of extra work to make up for a distraction. She needs a workable transition back to editing.

Sage: And she may discover that her original goal was unrealistic for the session. If she is on call and must respond constantly, uninterrupted editing may belong elsewhere in the day. Good design respects actual constraints. It doesn't require pretending those constraints have disappeared.

Ash: Third recall question. Leon removes his phone but still cannot begin the sales email. What should he check next?

Sage: Whether the task is clear and he has the information and skill needed to take its first step. Reducing distraction and knowing what to do are different problems.

Ash: I want to bring this into business for a moment. Suppose an owner wants to send invoices promptly. Saying be better with money doesn't identify a routine. What would an actual routine look like?

Sage: In a fictional service business, after marking a job complete, the owner opens the invoice template and checks the agreed details. Sending the invoice still requires accuracy and whatever approval the business needs. The habitual starting step is opening the relevant process at the moment the work is marked complete. We aren't teaching tax rules here; we're making an administrative action easier to initiate.

Ash: And if the details are scattered across messages, the habit won't fix that. The owner also needs a reliable place for the agreed price and customer information. That's another example of matching the routine to a functioning system.

Sage: Yes. A useful routine should help you perform a sound process. Automating a careless process can make the same mistake happen more consistently. Before making an action easy to repeat, make sure it's the action you actually want repeated.

Ash: Fourth recall question. What is the difference between establishing a starting habit and mastering the activity?

Sage: The starting habit helps you begin in a familiar situation. Mastery requires learning and practising the activity itself, with feedback. Opening the book and understanding its argument are separate achievements.

Ash: Let's finish with one change you can make today. Choose an ordinary action you want to begin more reliably, such as opening a book or starting a piece of work. Keep it small enough to set up now.

Sage: Complete this sentence: after this specific event, in this place, I will do this first action. Then prepare whatever that action needs. Put the book where you'll read it. Open the relevant file. Place the checklist where the task happens. Choose one arrangement, not a complete reinvention of your day.

Ash: If you're changing a distraction, identify where the unwanted action becomes easy. Move one trigger or add one practical barrier, and make the intended alternative available. Keep necessary calls or responsibilities in mind. We are designing an ordinary routine, not prescribing isolation from your life.

Sage: At the next opportunity, try the arrangement. If it breaks, locate the break: the cue didn't happen, the materials weren't ready, the action was unclear, or the setting was unsuitable. Repair that part. There is no need for a long diary or an experiment before you are allowed to start.

Ash: Fifth recall question. What is one reason a new intention can fail to change an established routine?

Sage: The familiar situation can still prompt the old response. An intention gives direction, but changing what is available and easy at the relevant moment can help you act on it.

Ash: The practical takeaway is specific: choose the first action, choose its cue, and prepare its setting. Then let the actual attempt tell you which part needs adjusting.
