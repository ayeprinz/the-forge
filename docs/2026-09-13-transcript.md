# [Critical Thinking] The First Test: How to Know What Helps

A foundation for The Forge: evaluate claims, distinguish observation from explanation, and practise with a worked example. Revised edition with clearer dialogue, recall questions and a reading companion approach.

**Sage:** Welcome to The Forge, an original learning programme spoken by two AI voices. We'll move through philosophy and the practical questions of a life, from work and money to history and spiritual traditions. Today we begin with a tool that helps you assess what you hear. What would give you a good reason to believe a claim? You don't need any earlier episode. We'll build the method here, then begin one small observation of an ordinary working habit.

**Ash:** What should someone be able to do by the end of this lesson?

**Sage:** A modest test. One clear question, a record you can inspect, and enough patience to admit when the answer is uncertain. Fourteen days can be a useful starting window for a low-risk habit. It isn't a universal scientific deadline, and it won't settle everything a book might claim.

**Ash:** So no previous reading is needed?

**Sage:** None. We'll explain the ideas as we go. The goal is to distinguish a claim from an observation, consider another explanation, and say what remains uncertain. Books and later lessons will give those habits more depth. Today is an introduction, with a practical example you can follow without a background in research.

**Ash:** So what makes a claim testable. Give me the criteria before the method.

**Sage:** For today's exercise, look for an observable outcome, a result that would count against your expectation, and a question small enough to examine without disrupting your life. The short time window is a practical constraint on this exercise, not the definition of a testable claim.

**Ash:** An observable outcome, a prediction that could be challenged, and a suitable method.

**Sage:** A claim might be testable yet need years, specialist equipment, or many people. We won't call it meaningless because it doesn't fit a notebook. We'll choose a smaller question that does fit, and mark the boundary around what it can answer.

**Ash:** Start with counting.

**Sage:** A useful prediction describes what you expect to observe under specified conditions. That might be a quantity, such as a delay in minutes, or a clearly described event. The important part is that you can distinguish what happened from what you expected. A prediction becomes less informative when it can be stretched to fit any outcome.

**Ash:** Give me a claim that fails that.

**Sage:** Your life will improve is too broad for this exercise. Improve in what respect, compared with what, and over what period? A useful observation needn't always be numerical, but its meaning must be clear enough that different outcomes can be distinguished.

**Ash:** And one that passes.

**Sage:** For example: keeping my phone out of reach during a planned writing session will reduce how long I take to begin. We would need to define out of reach, beginning, and the comparison. The sentence gives us something to develop into a question. It doesn't yet establish that the claim is true.

**Ash:** Counting sounds so small next to what these books are promising.

**Sage:** A record gives you something to inspect when your enthusiasm changes. Numbers can still be wrong. You can choose a flattering measure or omit an awkward day. That's why the definition and the recording rule matter as much as the tally.

**Ash:** Say that again.

**Sage:** The tally is useful because it leaves a trace. It doesn't become immune to error just because it's a number.

**Ash:** Can we make that concrete?

**Sage:** Imagine you plan a writing session for ten in the morning. You begin at ten twenty. The delay is twenty minutes. That is an observation with a clear definition, assuming the times were recorded accurately.

**Ash:** And the explanation?

**Sage:** Perhaps you were distracted by messages. Perhaps you were waiting for a file. Perhaps the planned time was unrealistic. Those possibilities don't become facts because one of them sounds persuasive.

**Ash:** So I write the delay first.

**Sage:** Yes, then the relevant context. If a file was unavailable, note it. If you don't know why you delayed, write that. The record can contain uncertainty without becoming useless.

**Ash:** What would the next observation add?

**Sage:** It might reveal that the same obstacle recurs. Or it might show that today was unusual. You need that opportunity for the pattern to change before you start describing a trait or a cause.

**Ash:** That's a modest start.

**Sage:** A useful one. We aren't diagnosing a personality from one morning. We're learning to describe an event accurately enough that tomorrow's event can be compared with it.

**Ash:** So give me the first job with a claim again.

**Sage:** For a practical claim, ask what observation would bear on it. A number may help, but some questions require a description or a historical record. Match the evidence to the question rather than forcing every kind of understanding into a tally.

**Ash:** Can we record internal states, such as how focused a session felt?

**Sage:** Yes. One option is a personal rating scale with clear anchors. You might describe what low and high focus mean before using it, then ask the same question at a consistent point after each session. That supports consistency. It does not make the rating an objective measure, or guarantee that you interpret the scale the same way every time.

**Ash:** It's still just me making up a number.

**Sage:** It's a personal rating with defined anchors. That can make your reporting more consistent, but it doesn't turn the feeling into an objective instrument. If you also have a timer or a timestamp for the behaviour you care about, keep that alongside the rating.

**Ash:** The written definition gives me something to check against if my interpretation changes.

**Sage:** Exactly. If you later improve the definition, keep the earlier version and record when it changed. That way you can tell whether two scores were produced using the same rule.

**Ash:** Second criterion. Could come out the other way.

**Sage:** Write what would make you reduce your confidence in this particular version of the claim. A single disappointing day might be noise. A pattern that contradicts a precise prediction deserves attention. The strength of the conclusion depends on how much opportunity the test had to detect an effect.

**Ash:** Say the sentence you'd write.

**Sage:** For example: if this prompt doesn't seem to help me start work across the planned sessions, I won't call this small trial evidence that it helps. I'll also record whether the comparison was too noisy to say much. That's more honest than making a fortnight the judge of every possible version of the idea.

**Ash:** And if I can't write it?

**Sage:** Then the statement may be expressing a value, a metaphor, or a claim that needs a better definition. We can discuss all of those. We just shouldn't treat every possible outcome as successful evidence for a prediction.

**Ash:** Where does that way of thinking come from?

**Sage:** One influential source is Karl Popper's discussion of falsifiability. The question is whether an account rules out any observations, rather than fitting everything after the event.

**Ash:** Give me an example.

**Sage:** Imagine someone says every success proves their method works, while every failure proves you didn't believe enough. If neither success nor failure can count against the explanation, those observations aren't testing it fairly.

**Ash:** The explanation always gets to win.

**Sage:** That's the problem. It doesn't settle every philosophical issue about the account, but it shows why those testimonials can't carry the evidential weight being placed on them.

**Ash:** Can a prediction be precise and still leave room for measurement error?

**Sage:** Certainly. Suppose your claim predicts a five-minute reduction in delay. A timer error, a different kind of task, or ordinary day-to-day variation can complicate the comparison. A fair test puts a prediction at risk while also checking whether the observations were capable of testing it. A surprising measurement is a reason to investigate, not an automatic verdict.

**Ash:** And if a claim about manifestation treats every possible outcome as confirmation?

**Sage:** Then the explanation may be protected from any adverse outcome. If success counts as evidence and failure is always explained away, ask what the author would actually accept as a challenge. Read the specific claim fairly before deciding that nothing could count against it.

**Ash:** Does that make the whole book useless?

**Sage:** That doesn't decide the value of an entire author. It asks us to separate a practice, an interpretation, and a claim about external events. We can investigate each on its own terms, without treating personal meaning as a laboratory result.

**Ash:** Fair chance.

**Sage:** A fair test allows observations to challenge a prediction. That is one way of assessing a claim. A poem, an ethical argument or a spiritual interpretation may call for a different kind of assessment.

**Ash:** Can you show why looking only for confirmation can miss an alternative?

**Sage:** Peter Wason's nineteen sixty paper used a number-rule task to examine how people test hypotheses. Here's a shortened illustration of that task. You hear two, four, six, and you're told that those numbers fit a rule. You may propose other triples and receive feedback.

**Ash:** How do I find out.

**Sage:** You propose your own triples, and he tells you yes or no, and you keep going until you're ready to announce the rule. So what do you propose?

**Ash:** Eight, ten, twelve.

**Sage:** Yes. And then?

**Ash:** Twenty, twenty two, twenty four.

**Sage:** Yes. And then a hundred, a hundred and two, a hundred and four. Yes. And by now you're sure the rule is even numbers going up in twos, so you announce it. And you're wrong. The rule was any three numbers in ascending order.

**Ash:** Oh.

**Sage:** Those examples all fit your guess, but also fit a broader rule. In Wason's task, participants often struggled to seek cases that could reveal that difference. This illustrates a testing problem; it doesn't mean every person always reasons that way in every setting.

**Ash:** Would three, five, nine distinguish those two rules?

**Sage:** Yes. That triple fits ascending order but not even numbers increasing by two. One, two, three would also distinguish those rules. A yes does not establish the final rule by itself, because other rules may fit. It tells you your original guess was too narrow. Then you keep testing alternatives.

**Ash:** It could feel uncomfortable to challenge an idea someone values.

**Sage:** It can. Start by stating the idea fairly and choosing a proportionate question. You can value a practice without pretending the explanation attached to it has already been established. The point is to improve understanding, not to manufacture a failure.

**Ash:** How do we choose a realistic observation period?

**Sage:** For this starting exercise, fourteen days is a manageable window. Some questions require much longer. The duration should follow the expected effect, the variation in the outcome, and how often you can observe it. A short test is a first look, not a shortcut to certainty.

**Ash:** Why use a small starting window at all?

**Sage:** Completion matters. A small plan you can maintain gives you a record to examine. But finishing a short plan doesn't guarantee that it contains enough information. We can finish it and still conclude that more observations are needed.

**Ash:** And if the result is still uncertain at the end?

**Sage:** The advantage is that you learn how to make a question precise and collect usable observations. You won't necessarily get twenty six settled answers a year. Some questions remain open. That isn't a failure of discipline; it's part of respecting the limits of the evidence.

**Ash:** What if the proposed effect takes months?

**Sage:** You can sometimes identify an earlier outcome, but only if there is a reason to expect it to change early. A real long-term effect doesn't have to produce a detectable change in the first week. Don't invent that requirement because it suits the diary.

**Ash:** Give me one.

**Sage:** For example, you could ask whether a short, familiar quiet pause before work changes how quickly you begin that session. That is a question about starting work that day. It doesn't stand in for a claim about long-term brain changes or every form of stress.

**Ash:** And if the fortnight shows nothing?

**Sage:** Then you have not found a clear effect in those observations. That may reflect a small effect, substantial variation, an unsuitable design, or no effect. You cannot choose between those explanations simply because the fortnight ended. Write down what the test could and could not detect before deciding how much the result should change your confidence.

**Ash:** That's the sentence I'd forget.

**Sage:** Put that limit on the same sheet as the prediction. It is easier to remember when it stays beside the result.

**Ash:** So the question comes before the test.

**Sage:** Yes. First decide what you are asking. Then consider what evidence could answer it. Sometimes the next step is a notebook, sometimes it is reading a source, and sometimes it is asking someone with the relevant expertise.

**Ash:** How does the number of opportunities to observe an outcome matter?

**Sage:** Choose an outcome you have enough chances to observe. More observations can help, but repeated days from the same person aren't automatically independent. A good or bad run can carry across days. We need to think about that when interpreting the pattern.

**Ash:** Example.

**Sage:** Opportunities may be too rare for a fortnight to tell you much. Starting a planned work session happens more often, so it can be a better first question. But it is a different outcome. A result about starting work cannot establish that a practice attracts opportunities.

**Ash:** So the smaller question needs its own conclusion.

**Sage:** A nearby question you can observe, with its own boundary. We aren't using a small answer as permission to announce a much larger one.

**Ash:** So what's actually on the card. Before day one.

**Sage:** Write the claim, the main outcome and its definition, when and how you will record it, the proposed comparison if one is suitable, what would count against the prediction, and what the design cannot establish. A feeling scale only needs anchors if you actually use one. These fields should fit the question, rather than becoming a ritual copied onto every subject.

**Ash:** Question, measure, recording rule, comparison, challenge and limits.

**Sage:** Those are planning prompts. Their usefulness depends on applying them appropriately. Filling six boxes does not make an unsuitable experiment informative.

**Ash:** Meaning?

**Sage:** It can help with ordinary habits, such as where you put your phone before a writing session. It isn't a licence to change medication, experiment with supplements, cut sleep, or make tax decisions from a fortnight of observations. Higher-stakes questions need the appropriate evidence and professional advice.

**Ash:** Could we examine an illustrative visualisation claim without assuming what any particular book says?

**Sage:** Take a general visualisation claim: imagining a successful work session before you start will help you begin. This is an illustrative claim for our discussion. We don't need to attribute it to one author's exact words to examine what it would predict.

**Ash:** How would that differ from a broad promise about circumstances changing?

**Sage:** If the promise is only that circumstances will rearrange themselves at some unspecified time, we don't yet have a prediction we can evaluate here. We need an outcome and a time frame. Otherwise almost any event could be presented as confirmation.

**Ash:** Show me.

**Sage:** The wording leaves important things unspecified. Which circumstances, what sort of change, and by when? Without those details, we can't say what this exercise would count as support or contradiction. That is a problem of definition before it is a problem of belief.

**Ash:** So I need to check the actual wording.

**Sage:** Yes. Read the claim carefully before narrowing it. A fair criticism addresses what someone actually proposed. If you replace it with a different claim, be explicit that you've designed your own question.

**Ash:** What smaller question could we investigate?

**Sage:** Ask whether this particular exercise affects the start of your next planned work session. That's a behavioural question. It's within reach of a timestamp, and it doesn't require us to settle a theory about the universe first.

**Ash:** But a manageable question is not necessarily the question the author meant.

**Sage:** Choose a question you can reach, but don't pretend that every larger claim depends on the outcome you happened to choose. The connection between a mechanism and a measure must be argued for, not assumed.

**Ash:** So a finding about starting work would not settle a claim about external circumstances.

**Sage:** Exactly. We are changing the question. If this exercise helps you begin a session, that is evidence about that outcome under these conditions. If it doesn't, it hasn't disproved every metaphysical claim someone might attach to visualisation.

**Ash:** All right. Second move.

**Sage:** Choose a primary outcome with a clear recording rule. For our example, minutes from the scheduled start to opening the work file. Use the same definition each time. A personal feeling score can be secondary, with its anchors defined before the first observation.

**Ash:** A timestamp measures an action. Does opening the file really mean doing useful work?

**Sage:** Not necessarily. A file timestamp leaves a trace, but the trace may be a poor measure of the outcome you care about. You could define beginning as opening the project and writing a first sentence. Keep that definition consistent. Don't switch to a more flattering measure after seeing the result.

**Ash:** So a clear measure still needs to match the claim.

**Sage:** That's a more precise hypothesis. We can gather preliminary observations in a fortnight. Whether they answer it depends on how much the behaviour varies and how the comparison is designed.

**Ash:** We should label our question as an adaptation, rather than attribute it to the book.

**Sage:** It's a different and narrower sentence. Keep both sentences visible so you don't confuse them at the end. A modest useful finding is enough. It doesn't need to certify the whole book.

**Ash:** Now the design. How do I stop the fortnight just being fourteen good days.

**Sage:** For an ordinary, reversible habit with an immediate effect, you could plan repeated comparison sessions. If you want exactly seven sessions in each condition, prepare seven cards of each type and shuffle them. Independent coin flips don't guarantee equal numbers. Whether that design suits the habit needs thought first.

**Ash:** Why not just alternate?

**Sage:** Alternation can line up with weekdays or predictable changes. Random assignment can reduce that problem, but it doesn't guarantee balance in a small sample. There is no universal two-day block rule. How long conditions last depends on how long their effects may last.

**Ash:** Why not just do it for two weeks and compare against how I was before? That's simpler.

**Sage:** A before-and-after comparison can mix the effect of the practice with whatever else changed over time. Your workload may change, or you may get used to the task. We need to consider plausible alternatives before assigning the improvement to one new habit.

**Ash:** Go on.

**Sage:** Regression to the mean is another possibility. When an unusually high or low measurement includes temporary variation, a later measurement may be less extreme even without an intervention. That is a statistical tendency under relevant conditions, not a promise that every difficult period will improve on its own.

**Ash:** What if someone starts a new routine just after an unusually bad week?

**Sage:** If you start after an unusually bad week, some apparent improvement might reflect ordinary variation. It might also reflect real changes in your life. The point is to consider those explanations, rather than assume the practice caused the whole difference.

**Ash:** That could make a testimonial hard to interpret.

**Sage:** Yes. A sincere account of improvement doesn't by itself reveal the cause. The person may have improved, and the proposed explanation may still be uncertain. We can respect the account while asking what comparison would help us understand it.

**Ash:** Does random assignment solve all of that?

**Sage:** It helps prevent you choosing the favourable days for your preferred condition. Over repeated assignments it can balance other influences, but fourteen days can still be uneven. Record the context and examine the order, rather than assuming the shuffle makes everything equal.

**Ash:** That's the whole reason for a control.

**Sage:** A comparison gives you a reference for what might have happened otherwise. It doesn't answer every causal question on its own. Its quality depends on how similar the conditions are apart from the thing you're trying to examine.

**Ash:** Now do a claim that actually has evidence behind it. I want to see the contrast.

**Sage:** One researched example is an implementation intention. Peter Gollwitzer and Paschal Sheeran reviewed plans that link a specific situation with a specific response. The form is: if situation X arises, then I will do Y. The question is whether that kind of plan improves follow-through compared with an intention alone.

**Ash:** So how would a general intention to write become one of those plans?

**Sage:** And the if then version being, when I close the studio door at seven, I'll open the session file. Same goal. One specifies the cue and the response and welds them together.

**Ash:** And the evidence.

**Sage:** Gollwitzer and Sheeran published a meta analysis in two thousand and six, in Advances in Experimental Social Psychology. Ninety four independent tests. The pooled effect came out around point six five, which sits between medium and large in that literature.

**Ash:** That's a lot of studies.

**Sage:** It's a reason to take the approach seriously, while checking how closely the studies match the behaviour you care about. A pooled result across varied studies is not a promise about your next work session.

**Ash:** How should a listener use that group result?

**Sage:** The group result is relevant evidence, but it doesn't determine your individual response. It also doesn't prove that differences between participants reflect stable personal treatment effects. People vary for many reasons, including measurement noise.

**Ash:** So even the good evidence doesn't settle it for me.

**Sage:** The literature gives you a starting expectation. A small personal record may add something, but it should be weighed alongside stronger evidence rather than automatically overruling it.

**Ash:** Do we always need a personal trial before using a well-supported idea?

**Sage:** No. For a low-cost decision, suitable existing evidence may be enough to try a sensible routine. You do not need to personally reproduce every study. If you investigate your own outcome, choose the duration from the question and expected variation. A weakly supported claim does not automatically deserve a shorter experiment, or any experiment at all.

**Ash:** How would I run that one.

**Sage:** For now, use a simple plan as a working example: when the scheduled writing session begins, open the project file and work on one defined section. Record whether you followed the plan and what happened. A comparison of repeated sessions needs a separate design, especially because learning a useful plan may carry into later sessions.

**Ash:** What can a consistent recording procedure help with?

**Sage:** Recording promptly, using a fixed rule, can reduce some opportunities to reinterpret the outcome. It isn't equivalent to blinding in a clinical trial. You may still remember which condition you were in.

**Ash:** Walk me through it.

**Sage:** Knowing what you hoped to achieve can influence a subjective rating. That doesn't make every rating worthless. It gives us a reason to choose the measure carefully and keep its limitations visible.

**Ash:** I'd catch myself.

**Sage:** You might notice some of that influence and miss some of it. Rather than relying on perfect self-awareness, use a fixed question and record the answer close to the event. Keep the raw entry, even if your interpretation changes later.

**Ash:** Could you illustrate that with a diary example?

**Sage:** Imagine a diary where you rate a session after reading a page about how powerful the exercise should feel. Then imagine rating it from the same short question before rereading that page. The second procedure removes one immediate prompt. It still doesn't erase everything you remember or expect.

**Ash:** But the person still knows what exercise they did.

**Sage:** That's an example, not a claim that the diary has become blinded. In a behavioural experiment, you often know exactly what you did. We should state that openly, rather than borrowing the authority of a blinded trial.

**Ash:** That's unsettling.

**Sage:** The useful habit is to separate observation from explanation where you can. Write the time or the rating first. Put the explanation in its own field. That makes it easier to revisit the evidence without losing the context.

**Ash:** Could expectations change the behaviour itself, as well as the rating?

**Sage:** There are several places expectations can enter. You can treat the conditions differently, record them differently, or analyse them differently. Those are separate problems. A timer may help with recording while doing nothing about unequal effort.

**Ash:** For example, putting more effort into the condition I favour?

**Sage:** If you try harder on the days you expect to work, the result may reflect that whole package. It may still be useful practically, but it won't isolate the special mechanism you hoped to test.

**Ash:** So I'm both the person doing it and the person judging it.

**Sage:** Yes, which makes the separation difficult. A fixed procedure helps. An independent record can help. Neither guarantees an unbiased result. We need to carry that limitation into the conclusion.

**Ash:** Pre registration. Why does that exist.

**Sage:** Preregistration makes the intended question and analysis visible before the results are known. It helps readers distinguish an advance prediction from an explanation developed after inspecting the data. That improves transparency, but it does not make a weak design strong or prevent every kind of mistake.

**Ash:** Who demonstrated the problem?

**Sage:** Joseph Simmons, Leif Nelson and Uri Simonsohn, two thousand and eleven, in Psychological Science. The paper is called False Positive Psychology. They ran real experiments with real people to show how easy it is to produce an impossible result using ordinary, respectable analysis choices.

**Ash:** Give me the impossible one.

**Sage:** In one demonstration, the researchers used choices in analysis to produce an apparent relationship between listening to a song and participants being younger. That does not mean a song changed anyone's age. The point was to show how a misleading result can emerge from choices that might each sound defensible in isolation.

**Ash:** Younger.

**Sage:** The song didn't make anyone younger. The apparent result illustrated how flexible analysis and selective reporting can generate a misleading finding. That demonstration gives us a reason to specify the analysis before looking for an impressive result.

**Ash:** So the flexibility is the problem.

**Sage:** They called these choices researcher degrees of freedom. Decisions about which observations, outcomes and analyses to report can increase the chance of a misleading finding, especially when the choices are guided by results. Flexible analysis is not inherently dishonest. The problem is hiding the flexibility while presenting the selected result as a clean test of an advance prediction.

**Ash:** And in my fortnight, the degrees of freedom are.

**Sage:** Possible choices include when the observation period begins, which sessions to exclude, which outcome to emphasise, and whether to report an average or a selected subset. Some changes can be justified. Preserve the original plan and explain the changes so their influence can be assessed.

**Ash:** Unless I decide them first.

**Sage:** Decide what you can in advance, then document departures. A dated plan helps distinguish intended analysis from later exploration. There is more to formal preregistration than a sentence, but that distinction is useful even in a personal notebook.

**Ash:** And there's a name for doing it backwards.

**Sage:** That reversal is often called hypothesising after the results are known. It can make exploration look like a prediction that survived a test. The solution isn't to stop noticing surprises. It's to label when the idea arose.

**Ash:** Which in my life would be.

**Sage:** You test whether the exercise helps you start work. That outcome barely changes, but another recorded outcome looks better. That may be worth investigating. It doesn't turn the original prediction into a success, and it shouldn't be reported as though you expected it all along.

**Ash:** I can see how a surprising result could become the story I remember.

**Sage:** That is the risk we are trying to reduce. Naming a main outcome in advance gives the exercise a clear question. Reporting the other observations honestly keeps a selected success from hiding the rest of the record.

**Ash:** But what if I notice something real that I wasn't looking for.

**Sage:** Treat it as a new possibility to investigate. Check whether it is already explained by good evidence, whether it could reflect chance or a measurement problem, and what comparison would help. You may need a new study, more observations or a different method. There is no rule that every surprise requires another fortnight.

**Ash:** Why does measuring several things make it worse. That feels like more information.

**Sage:** If you search many outcomes for a striking result, you create more chances to find a pattern in noise. It isn't guaranteed that one will look impressive, and the measures may be related. The danger is presenting the selected result without disclosing the search that produced it.

**Ash:** And I'd report the impressive one.

**Sage:** That selective account can be misleading. Research can include planned secondary outcomes that are informative in their own right, with appropriate analysis. The essential distinction is between what was planned and what was selected after seeing the data. Report the whole relevant picture, including results that did not fit the preferred story.

**Ash:** So additional information is useful if we explain how it was selected and analysed.

**Sage:** You can measure additional outcomes, but label them and report them honestly. Naming a primary outcome helps clarify the main question. It doesn't remove every statistical issue from the extra analyses.

**Ash:** Generate in one test, confirm in the next.

**Sage:** Separate exploration from confirmation. That makes an unexpected observation easier to investigate without overstating it. An independent check can still be inconclusive or introduce its own limitations.

**Ash:** You said a log has to be evidence rather than a story.

**Sage:** A useful log lets another reading of the same record remain possible. It separates the observations from the story you currently tell about them.

**Ash:** What makes a log a story.

**Sage:** Explanations are useful, but mixing them into the observation can hide what was actually recorded. Keep a field for the outcome and another for context. Later you can compare interpretations without rewriting the observation itself.

**Ash:** Which fields would help preserve that distinction?

**Sage:** Use a consistent set of fields: date, planned condition if there is one, whether you followed it, and the outcome. Add a separate context field for things such as an interrupted session. Decide those fields before you see which days look good.

**Ash:** Where would interruptions and other context go?

**Sage:** Context is data too when you record it consistently. It can help explain why a simple comparison is misleading. The key is not to invent convenient exclusions after noticing that they make your favourite condition win.

**Ash:** Rules for keeping it.

**Sage:** Choose a recording time close to the event. Mark missed observations as missing. If you reconstruct something later, label it as reconstructed rather than treating it as a contemporaneous record. Keep planned and actual behaviour distinct.

**Ash:** Why does back filling matter so much. It's a day I remember.

**Sage:** Recall can be imperfect, so a late entry should carry that limitation. A timestamp from the work file may help reconstruct when you began, but it won't tell you everything about the session. Keep the source of the observation clear.

**Ash:** Can you give an example of a memory and a timestamp disagreeing?

**Sage:** Suppose you remember a productive afternoon but the file history shows only a brief edit. Neither record contains the whole story. The useful response is to retain both and say what each can establish, instead of quietly choosing the one you prefer.

**Ash:** So neither source should silently replace the other.

**Sage:** That example shows why an early note can be helpful. It doesn't mean every later memory is false. It means later reconstruction and direct recording should not be treated as interchangeable sources.

**Ash:** And a missed day I'm tempted to leave out.

**Sage:** Selective omission can distort the picture. If you exclude days, state a rule in advance where possible, retain the raw record, and report what changed. A difficult day isn't automatically invalid, and neither is every recorded day automatically comparable.

**Ash:** That's the researcher degrees of freedom thing again, in my kitchen.

**Sage:** Keep a line for each planned observation, including missing entries and departures from the plan. Sometimes the main finding is that the procedure was impractical. Sometimes the data are too incomplete to interpret. Don't force either into a verdict about the practice itself.

**Ash:** Now the question I actually can't answer. How big does a difference have to be before I believe it. Sample of one.

**Sage:** There isn't a universal threshold that turns fourteen days into proof. Consider the size of the difference, the variation, the order of the observations, and whether the design could answer the question. We can inspect those without pretending a visual rule is a statistical test.

**Ash:** How do I even know what my noise is.

**Sage:** Look at how your observations vary within comparable sessions. That gives you context, but the total range isn't a threshold below which every effect is meaningless. A difference smaller than the range can still be real. With few noisy observations, it may simply be hard to distinguish from chance.

**Ash:** Is an average enough to describe that variation?

**Sage:** Examine the individual observations as well as averages. Two conditions can share the same minimum and maximum and still differ. Conversely, different averages may reflect a few unusual days. Neither the range nor the mean tells the whole story on its own.

**Ash:** That's a picture I can actually draw.

**Sage:** Plot the observations in time order and mark the condition. Look for trends, gaps and unusual values. The picture helps you ask better questions; overlapping dots don't prove that there is no effect, and separated dots don't remove every alternative explanation.

**Ash:** Give me a number. I want a rule of thumb.

**Sage:** With only a few sessions per condition, small effects are often difficult to assess. How difficult depends on the design and the variability. There is no honest universal rule that the difference must exceed the day-to-day range. For a meaningful decision, get an appropriate analysis or collect more suitable observations.

**Ash:** What do I do with a small one.

**Sage:** Call it uncertain when the evidence is weak. A small observed difference is not the same as evidence of no effect. Ask whether the test had enough precision to distinguish an effect you care about from ordinary variation.

**Ash:** That's a hard verdict to accept when I've done fourteen days of work.

**Sage:** You can have completed good work and still have an inconclusive result. That distinction keeps effort from becoming a reason to exaggerate what the data show.

**Ash:** So the possible endings are.

**Sage:** A promising pattern that deserves another check. Evidence against the specific effect you tested, if the test was precise enough. Or an inconclusive result. In a short informal diary, that last category will often be the most defensible.

**Ash:** It can be tempting to avoid that third answer.

**Sage:** Yes. An inconclusive result can feel unsatisfying after putting in the work. But choosing a strong conclusion because it feels better would undo the purpose of collecting the observations.

**Ash:** Is testing on one person a real method, though, or a hobby.

**Sage:** It's a real method with a literature. Gordon Guyatt and colleagues at McMaster published on randomised trials in individual patients in the New England Journal of Medicine in nineteen eighty six. A single patient, repeated blinded crossovers between drug and placebo, to work out whether that patient responds. Clinicians still use the design.

**Ash:** That surprises me.

**Sage:** An individual trial can help answer an individual question when the conditions are suitable. Clinical trials of that kind require clinical judgement. Our exercise stays with ordinary working habits, and won't borrow medical authority for a casual diary.

**Ash:** Can a published finding fail an independent check?

**Sage:** Yes. A relevant example is the retroactive recall experiment discussed by Stuart Ritchie, Richard Wiseman and Christopher French in their replication paper. The original claim concerned whether practice occurring later could influence earlier recall. We can examine what happened when that specific result was tested again.

**Ash:** In a serious journal.

**Sage:** Publication is a stage of scrutiny, not the end of it. A result can pass review and still depend on chance, an unrecognised feature of the procedure or choices in analysis. Those are possibilities to investigate, rather than explanations to assume.

**Ash:** Did anyone check.

**Sage:** Stuart Ritchie, Richard Wiseman and Christopher French reported three preregistered replication attempts of the retroactive recall experiment in PLoS One in twenty twelve. Those attempts did not find a statistically significant effect. That is the result we can report, without turning it into a verdict on every spiritual question.

**Ash:** What conclusion can we draw from those attempts?

**Sage:** Those replication attempts are evidence about that particular experiment. They don't settle every claim about spirituality. They show why a published result should be examined and tested again rather than treated as a final verdict.

**Ash:** Has replication been examined across a larger set of psychology studies?

**Sage:** The Open Science Collaboration, twenty fifteen, in Science. Two hundred and seventy authors redid one hundred psychology studies. Ninety seven of the originals had reported significant results. Thirty six of the replications did.

**Ash:** Ninety seven to thirty six.

**Sage:** The replication effects were smaller on average. This concerned a selected set of studies, not every finding in psychology. Statistical significance is also only one way of comparing results. The project gives a reason for careful interpretation, not for declaring the whole subject empty.

**Ash:** Say it plainly.

**Sage:** A striking result deserves scrutiny and independent checking. Surprise alone doesn't tell you whether it is a discovery or an error. Ask whether the observation survives another fair opportunity to fail.

**Ash:** And Feynman's line.

**Sage:** In his nineteen seventy four Caltech address, Cargo Cult Science, Richard Feynman argued for intellectual honesty that includes checking how you might be misleading yourself. I'm paraphrasing the principle here, rather than treating a memorable quotation as a substitute for a method.

**Ash:** The easiest person to fool.

**Sage:** The demand is to present the weaknesses of your case as well as its strengths. That includes information an audience would need to judge the conclusion differently from you.

**Ash:** Where's the image from.

**Sage:** Feynman used an analogy about copying the visible appearance of an operation while missing what makes it work. We can apply that distinction to a diary: dates and columns can resemble research without providing a fair comparison.

**Ash:** The form without the thing that made it work.

**Sage:** A page of numbers is the appearance. The work is deciding what the numbers measure, how the comparison was made, and which other explanations remain. That's the lesson we need from the analogy, without leaning on it as a historical account.

**Ash:** Can we apply that principle to checking surprising numbers?

**Sage:** Consider an invented example. You expect a writing routine to produce twenty useful minutes. One day it produces ten, and you inspect every interruption. Another produces thirty, and you accept it without checking. The unequal scrutiny can shape your conclusion.

**Ash:** How should the checking rule change?

**Sage:** Apply the same checking rule to both. An interruption on a good day deserves the same note as an interruption on a bad one. Otherwise your preferred conclusion decides which observations receive extra investigation.

**Ash:** The same scrutiny should apply whether the result is convenient or awkward.

**Sage:** A prewritten rule helps reveal that inconsistency. It doesn't remove judgement, but it gives you something outside today's preference to compare your judgement against.

**Ash:** And what if the measure itself is wrong?

**Sage:** Then even consistent recording can mislead. Suppose the timer measures how long a file is open, including an hour when you left the room. It measures an open file, not focused work. Fixing that definition is more useful than collecting another month of the wrong quantity.

**Ash:** So collecting more of the wrong measurement would not fix it.

**Sage:** Check what the instrument actually records. A count can be precise about the wrong thing. Your definition is part of the test.

**Ash:** So what does this change for me. Tomorrow morning, in practice.

**Sage:** It gives you a way to approach the next claim without either swallowing it whole or dismissing it on instinct. Some questions belong in reading and discussion. Some need established research. A few can be explored through a small personal record.

**Ash:** So I don't need to settle the whole library first.

**Sage:** No. Ask a precise question and choose a method suited to it. Reading can answer many questions that your own experiment cannot. Your notebook adds observations about your circumstances; it doesn't replace the accumulated work of everyone else.

**Ash:** And the books that can't be turned into a prediction.

**Sage:** They may deserve another kind of reading: historical, ethical, literary, or philosophical. A text can matter without making a prediction about tomorrow's work session. Recognising its purpose can make the reading more useful.

**Ash:** With so much to learn, how do we avoid turning every book into a separate experiment?

**Sage:** Prioritise questions by relevance, cost and what you could realistically learn. You won't test an entire library in a year. Many questions are better answered by consulting good sources, and some don't call for an experiment at all.

**Ash:** How would you decide which question deserves attention first?

**Sage:** Prioritise a question when it matters to a decision you actually face, when getting it wrong has a meaningful cost, and when better information could change your choice. You might find that information in an existing source. The goal is an informed decision, not a growing collection of experiments.

**Ash:** So relevance and the quality of available evidence guide the next step.

**Sage:** Yes. A question can be interesting without being urgent. You can also read a book to understand a tradition, argument or story without adopting its practical advice. Learning and committing to a practice are separate decisions.

**Ash:** And different subjects need different methods.

**Sage:** Use the method selectively. A familiar work routine may be suitable. Medication, supplements, unsafe exercise changes and financial obligations are not beginner diary experiments. The question decides the method, rather than every subject being squeezed into a fortnight.

**Ash:** What is the simplest first exercise?

**Sage:** For a first attempt, record a normal work session without changing your health routine. Write when you meant to begin, when you actually began, and what counted as beginning. This teaches you whether your measurement is usable before you start claiming an effect.

**Ash:** How would the difficulty change for a question about social media posting times?

**Sage:** Posting hour is a harder case and you should know why before you run it. The numbers on a post move by enormous amounts for reasons nothing to do with the hour. What the song was. Whether the platform pushed you. Whether someone big shared it. That's huge noise around a small effect.

**Ash:** Would comparing a few different posts establish the best hour?

**Sage:** You would need enough comparable posts to investigate timing, and content differences could still dominate. A short sequence of unrelated posts won't isolate the effect of the hour. First decide whether you can make a fair comparison at all.

**Ash:** So choose a question where the observations can genuinely help.

**Sage:** A low-cost work routine is a more manageable starting point. Keep the question focused on that session, and be clear about what your measure does and doesn't capture.

**Ash:** And if someone is interested in a quiet routine before work?

**Sage:** A quiet routine might be pleasant, or seem to help you begin work. Keep those possibilities separate from a medical claim. If you want to evaluate evidence about clinical outcomes, we need the relevant research and its limitations. Today's exercise stays with an ordinary work session.

**Ash:** That keeps the first step manageable.

**Sage:** Good. We'll keep the first action small enough to do and clear enough to inspect. No promise that every uncertainty will disappear in two weeks.

**Ash:** Can we put the ideas together in a complete example before the recall questions?

**Sage:** Let's use a fictional learner called Alex. These are invented observations for teaching, not research findings. Alex plans four comparable writing sessions to check whether the recording procedure is usable. The question is whether putting a phone out of reach might help with beginning work. Four sessions cannot establish that it does. They can reveal whether beginning has been defined clearly enough to measure.

**Ash:** What does Alex write before the first session?

**Sage:** The planned start is ten in the morning. Beginning means opening the project and writing the first sentence intended for that task. The outcome is the delay between the planned start and that sentence. Alex records the time promptly, notes where the phone was, and keeps a separate field for interruptions. That is a measurement pilot. It is not yet a carefully designed comparison of phone conditions.

**Ash:** What happens in the four sessions?

**Sage:** In the first, the phone is on the desk and the delay is twelve minutes. In the second, the phone is outside the room and the delay is three minutes. In the third, the phone is outside the room and the delay is fifteen minutes because a required file is unavailable. In the fourth, the phone is on the desk and the delay is four minutes. Keep those four observations visible rather than selecting the two that make the best story.

**Ash:** The first two sessions make the phone idea look convincing. The other two complicate it.

**Sage:** Exactly. You could say the delay fell from twelve minutes to three when the phone moved. Those two entries really say that. But reporting only those entries hides the rest. Across all four, the two phone-on-desk delays average eight minutes. The two phone-outside delays average nine minutes. That arithmetic describes the observations. It does not establish that moving the phone makes people slower.

**Ash:** Would we exclude the session when the file was missing?

**Sage:** First ask whether the definition and plan covered that situation. Alex can retain the entry, explain the interruption and show how the description changes with and without it. Excluding an awkward result silently would conceal a decision that matters. Equally, blindly treating an unavailable task as comparable to an available one would ignore relevant context. The record needs both the observation and the explanation.

**Ash:** What if there was no exclusion rule in advance?

**Sage:** Then label the decision as made after seeing the observations. The pilot has revealed a design problem: the work must actually be available for delay to mean the same thing across sessions. That is useful learning. It would be a mistake to repair the rule and then present the earlier observations as though they had been collected under the improved plan.

**Ash:** What is the honest conclusion?

**Sage:** Alex has not established a phone effect. There are too few sessions, no planned random assignment, and an important difference in whether the task was available. Alex has learned how to define a start, record a delay and notice a comparison problem. A useful next step could be improving the procedure and consulting existing research on distraction. It does not have to be another experiment immediately.

**Ash:** Could Alex still choose to put the phone outside the room?

**Sage:** Yes, if that is a convenient, low-cost preference. A practical choice does not need to be announced as a scientific discovery. Alex could say: I prefer this arrangement and will see whether it remains useful. That is different from saying: these four sessions prove why the arrangement works. Keep confidence in the explanation separate from the freedom to make an ordinary reversible choice.

**Ash:** How does that transfer to reading a book about history?

**Sage:** The habit of asking for support transfers. The notebook experiment usually does not. If a book claims that a particular event occurred, investigate the documents, their dates, provenance and relationship to other evidence. Several authors repeating one account are not necessarily several independent sources. A personal feeling of recognition while reading cannot establish that the historical event occurred.

**Ash:** And a book presenting a spiritual interpretation?

**Sage:** First understand what kind of claim it makes. An interpretation may be evaluated for its coherence, context and meaning within a tradition. A claim about a measurable physical effect also needs relevant empirical evidence. A passage can contain both. Reading it carefully means identifying the distinction rather than declaring the whole text either proven science or worthless.

**Ash:** So Forge can give me a starting framework, and reading the books can extend or challenge it.

**Sage:** Yes. Before returning to a topic, try explaining one idea from memory, then check it against the book or lesson. Notice what you omitted, misunderstood or need to qualify. In a study of vocabulary learning, Jeffrey Karpicke and Henry Roediger found that repeated retrieval supported delayed recall more than continued study alone after initial learning. That specific finding supports including recall practice; it does not promise mastery of every subject.

**Ash:** Is that automatically memory reconsolidation?

**Sage:** No. Reconsolidation refers to processes through which a reactivated memory can become unstable and then stabilise again. Reviews describe conditions that affect whether this occurs; retrieval does not always destabilise a memory. We cannot identify that process just because a second reading feels clearer. For learning here, the practical instruction is to recall, check, correct and revisit. We can use that routine without claiming to observe a particular brain mechanism.

**Ash:** What if I cannot remember enough to explain the idea?

**Sage:** Use a smaller question, then check the answer. For today's topic, start with: what is the difference between recording a delay and explaining its cause? A partial answer reveals where you need support. Read the relevant part again, correct the answer, and return to it later. You do not need to pass a test before being allowed to read. The questions help direct attention.

**Ash:** Let us try the five questions now.

**Sage:** Take a moment to answer each before hearing the explanation. You can pause longer if you need it. The purpose is to practise the distinctions, not to judge how quickly you speak.

**Ash:** One. What makes an observation useful for testing a prediction?

*[Seven-second thinking pause]*

**Sage:** It should specify an observable outcome and a prediction that could be challenged. The method and duration must suit the question. A fortnight is a practical starting exercise, not a rule that every real claim has to obey.

**Ash:** Two. Why ask what would reduce your confidence in a claim?

*[Seven-second thinking pause]*

**Sage:** Ask what outcome would reduce your confidence. If every possible observation confirms the explanation, the observation isn't distinguishing that explanation from alternatives. A good test creates a fair opportunity to learn either way.

**Ash:** Three. Does recording a rating before looking at the schedule genuinely blind you to a behaviour you remember performing?

*[Seven-second thinking pause]*

**Sage:** Prompt, consistent recording can reduce reinterpretation, but it doesn't genuinely blind you to a behaviour you remember doing. State that limit and use less subjective measures when appropriate. Keep the raw record and date any correction.

**Ash:** Four. How big does a difference have to be before a sample of one should move you.

*[Seven-second thinking pause]*

**Sage:** There's no universal threshold. Consider the design, variation and precision. Fourteen noisy observations may be inconclusive. Overlap doesn't prove absence of an effect, and a strong-looking pattern doesn't eliminate alternative causes.

**Ash:** Five. What makes a log evidence rather than a story.

*[Seven-second thinking pause]*

**Sage:** Defined fields, observations recorded close to the event, missing entries labelled and corrections visible. Context belongs in a separate field. The log remains useful because it preserves what happened rather than quietly adapting to the story you prefer.

**Ash:** Then give me the one thing. Today.

**Sage:** Today you make one observation sheet and record one ordinary work session. No new health practice, no supplement experiment, no complicated comparison. First learn whether you can measure the behaviour you care about.

**Ash:** Line by line.

**Sage:** Write the date and the session you planned. Define what beginning means, such as opening the project and writing a first line. Record the planned start time and the actual start. Keep a separate space for interruptions or anything that makes the entry hard to interpret.

**Ash:** What should I write about the limit of that one entry?

**Sage:** Then write the claim you might investigate later, and a sentence about what today's single observation cannot establish. It cannot tell you what caused the delay or whether a new routine would help. That boundary is part of the exercise.

**Ash:** After the card.

**Sage:** Save the sheet with its date. Keep the original if you later improve the design. You don't need to share it publicly. The point is to give your future judgement a record it didn't invent after seeing the outcome.

**Ash:** Day one.

**Sage:** Record the session close to when it happens. Tomorrow's lesson can build on that distinction between an observation and the story attached to it. You don't need a dramatic result today. You need one usable entry.

**Ash:** One clearly defined observation, with its limits written beside it.

**Sage:** One honest entry is a beginning. Over time, the value comes from asking better questions, checking the record and changing your mind when the evidence calls for it. That's the work this programme is here to support.
